    The history

    A short history of Black horror

    The scholar Robin R. Means Coleman, whose book Horror Noire is the definitive study of the subject, draws a line that explains almost everything in the charts above. There is Blacks in horror: Black characters placed inside stories that were never about them. And there is Black horror: films "typically created and written by African-Americans" that "really focus on Blackness and Black life and culture." For most of the last century, cinema offered the first. Only recently has it offered the second.

    1915 – 1940s

    The monster and the servant

    Black horror begins, uncomfortably, with a film that isn't usually filed as horror at all. D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation (1915) cast Black men, many of them white actors in blackface, as predatory threats to white womanhood, and celebrated the Ku Klux Klan as their vanquisher. "Some don't understand Birth of a Nation as a horror film," Coleman says, "but African Americans absolutely do." The Black filmmaker Oscar Micheaux answered it in 1920 with Within Our Gates, which depicted the lynching of Black Americans instead.

    When Hollywood's horror boom arrived in the 1930s and 40s, Black people appeared in two forms: as "savage" set dressing in jungle and voodoo pictures, which exoticised Caribbean and African religion as something monstrous, or as frightened comic servants. In King of the Zombies (1941), the gifted comedian Mantan Moreland plays a valet who spends the film convinced the mansion is haunted. Audiences were invited to laugh at his fear while the white leads investigated.

    Away from the studios, a parallel cinema of race films, made for Black audiences and screened in Black theatres, told different stories. Spencer Williams's Son of Ingagi (1940), which premiered at Detroit's Warfield Theater, was the first science-fiction horror film with an all-Black cast.

    Key term
    Exoticism. Presenting another culture as strange, primitive or dangerous for the entertainment of an outside audience. Early zombie films took Haitian Vodou and turned it into a source of cheap fright.

    1968

    The hero who gets shot

    George A. Romero's Night of the Living Dead cast Duane Jones as Ben, the calm, capable man who holds a farmhouse together against the dead. The part wasn't written for a Black actor, and Romero cast Jones simply because he gave the best audition, then left the script unchanged. That decision made the ending unbearable: Ben survives the night, only to be shot dead by a white posse who mistake him for a ghoul. Released months after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., the film was read by critics as an allegory of the violence of the civil rights era, whether Romero intended it or not.

    Key term
    Allegory. A story whose surface events stand for something else. Zombies are rarely just zombies: they have stood for consumerism, contagion, conformity and, here, the mob.

    1970s

    Blaxploitation's monsters

    In the early 1970s, studios discovered that Black audiences would pay to see Black heroes, and a wave of low-budget genre films followed. Horror took its share: Blacula (1972), in which an African prince is cursed by Count Dracula and awakens in modern Los Angeles; Blackenstein; Sugar Hill (1974), whose heroine raises the dead to avenge her murdered fiancé; and Abby (1974), an Exorcist variation pulled from cinemas after Warner Bros. sued.

    The films were popular and divisive in equal measure. The publicist Junius Griffin coined the deliberately derogatory label blaxploitation, and the NAACP, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and the National Urban League joined forces as the Coalition Against Blaxploitation, arguing the films sold stereotypes back to the community they claimed to celebrate. The tension was real: these were often the only films in which Black actors led, yet the money and the control largely sat elsewhere.

    The era's masterpiece slipped through almost unseen. Bill Gunn's Ganja & Hess (1973), an art-house meditation on addiction, faith and vampirism, was selected for Critics' Week at Cannes, then pulled from distribution by its producers and sold off, resurfacing in a drastically recut version called Blood Couple that Gunn disowned. Restored decades later, it was added to the US National Film Registry in 2024.

    Key term
    Commodification. Turning an identity or culture into a product to be sold. The central criticism of blaxploitation was that it commodified Blackness without handing Black artists the power to define it.

    1980 – 2000s

    Helpers, sacrifices and sidekicks

    As horror went mainstream, Black characters became supporting players whose purpose, in Coleman's words, was "to support the white leads." The archetype is Dick Hallorann in The Shining (1980): a gentle man with psychic gifts who crosses the country to rescue a white child and is the only person the killer manages to murder. In 2001, Spike Lee popularised a name for this figure, the Magical Negro: a supporting character, often with mystical wisdom, who exists to aid the white protagonist and has no story of his own.

    Bernard Rose's Candyman (1992) gave horror one of its great Black monsters, Tony Todd's hook-handed ghost, but told his story through the eyes of a white graduate student studying Chicago's Cabrini-Green estate. Black filmmakers pushed back from the margins. Rusty Cundieff's anthology Tales from the Hood (1995) aimed horror squarely at police corruption, domestic abuse, racism and gang violence. And by 1997 the trope was so familiar that Scream 2 could open with a Black couple mocking horror's whiteness in a cinema, moments before both are killed. The joke had become the joke.

    Key term
    The gaze. Whose eyes the camera looks through. A film can feature Black characters while still being told entirely from a white point of view, which shapes who the audience fears, pities and roots for.

    2017 – now

    From Blacks in horror to Black horror

    Jordan Peele's Get Out cost $4.5 million and took more than $255 million worldwide. Peele, who curated a season of films called "The Art of the Social Thriller" around its release, made the horror out of something Black audiences already knew: the polite, smiling danger of white liberal spaces. Its central image, the Sunken Place, is in Peele's words a metaphor for marginalisation: "No matter how hard we scream, the system silences us." Scholars have connected it to W. E. B. Du Bois's idea of double consciousness, the sense of always seeing yourself through the eyes of others. Peele became the first Black writer to win the Oscar for Best Original Screenplay.

    What followed was a genuine movement. Us (2019) made a Black family the unremarkable heart of a mainstream horror. Remi Weekes's British-made His House (2020) turned the asylum system into a haunted house. Nia DaCosta's Candyman (2021) reclaimed the legend for the people of Cabrini-Green and opened at number one in America, the first film by a Black woman to do so. The Blackening (2023) turned the old trope into its whole premise, and Ryan Coogler's Sinners (2025) took $370 million with a vampire story steeped in Delta blues.

    Key term
    Subversion. Using a genre's rules against themselves. Black horror after 2017 repeatedly takes the familiar beats, the dinner party, the family holiday, the cabin, and reveals the racial anxiety hiding underneath.

    The analysis

    What the numbers mean

    The data complicates the joke without dismissing it. In these films, a Black character was the very first to die only three times before 2017. Literally, the trope is an exaggeration. But exaggeration is how memory records a pattern, and the pattern was real: Black characters were marginal, positioned around the story rather than inside it, and 13 of the 22 whose fate is clear did not survive. They died helping, guarding, warning and rescuing, which is to say they died as the Magical Negro and the sacrificial sidekick were always written to die.

    After 2017, survival rises to 26 of 35. It would be easy to credit a change of heart in Hollywood, but the timeline points somewhere more specific: to authorship. The rise in survival coincides with Black writers and directors telling stories from a Black point of view, exactly the shift Coleman describes from Blacks in horror to Black horror. The caveat matters, since the newer films here lean towards Black-led stories by design, but that is also the argument. Who survives a horror film has always depended on whose story it is, and that depends on who is telling it.

    The method

    I picked 27 well-known horror films with Black characters in the main cast, 15 from before Get Out and 12 from after. For each one I read the plot summary on Wikipedia and logged who dies, in what order, and what happens to every Black main character. Villains and monsters aren't counted as victims. A character counts as Black if the actor is publicly identified as Black; where that wasn't clear, I left them out. Three films (Alien vs. Predator, Get Out and Candyman 2021) are left out of the "who died first" count because the first victim isn't clear from the source.

    This is a hand-picked set, not every horror film ever made, and the newer half leans towards Black-led films. That is part of the story, but it means the survival numbers describe these films, not the whole genre. The data is here and here if you want to check my work.
